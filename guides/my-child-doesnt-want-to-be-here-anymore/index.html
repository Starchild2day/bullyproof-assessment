My Child Doesn't Want to Be Here Anymore

🚨 If You Need Help Right Now

Call 911 if your child is in immediate danger.

Call or text 988 — the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline. Free, confidential, 24 hours a day, across the United States and its territories. Chat is also available online.

Text HOME to 741741 — Crisis Text Line. A trained crisis counselor, by text message.

These are United States services. If you are outside the US, use your local emergency number.

All of these appear again, in full, at the end of this article. They are repeated here at the top on purpose. A parent reading this in the first hours of something like this should not have to reach the bottom of a page to find a phone number.

Quick Help The 45-second version

1. The Situation
Your child has said something that stopped you cold. Maybe they said they don't want to be here anymore. Maybe they said everyone would be better off without them. Maybe it was quieter than that — a comment about not being around next year, a sudden calm after weeks of despair, giving things away. Whatever form it took, you heard it, and something in you knows this is different from the other hard moments in this arc.

2. The Three Things That Matter Most, Right Now
- Ask them directly. Clearly and calmly. Ask whether they are thinking about ending their life. The evidence does not support the fear that asking plants the idea — a meta-analysis across thirteen studies found that asking about suicidal risk did not increase distress or suicidal behavior [4].
- Stay with them. Do not leave your child alone while they are in this state. Presence is the intervention available to you right now.
- Get professional help today. Not this week. Today. A crisis line will help you decide what level of care is needed, and they will stay on the line with you [5].

One thing comes before all three. If your child has already attempted suicide, is actively trying to harm themselves, cannot stay safe, or is in immediate danger, call 911 (or your local emergency number) right away. Do not wait, and do not try to manage it at home. A crisis line is for guidance when the situation is being managed. 911 is for when it is not.

3. What Not to Do
Do not panic in front of them. Do not lecture, bargain, or make promises about secrecy. Do not tell them they don't mean it. Do not leave them alone to "cool off."

4. How to Ask
Sit down. Stay calm. Use plain words.

"I need to ask you something, and I'm not going to be upset with the answer. Are you thinking about ending your life?"

Then stop talking and wait.

5. If They Say Yes
Say four things and nothing else:

  • "Thank you for telling me. I know that was hard."
  • "I'm not going anywhere."
  • "You are not in trouble, and you're not going to be alone in this."
  • "We're going to get help today. Not next week. Today."

Then call. A crisis line, your child's doctor, or emergency services. Whoever is fastest [5][6][7].

6. If They Say No, but You're Not Convinced
Seek help even after a denial if concerns remain. Trust your instinct over their answer. Stay with them, keep the door open, and call a professional for guidance today anyway.

The principle to hold onto: seek help even after a denial, if concerns remain. A "no" does not cancel what you saw and heard. Connecting a professional on the same day is not an overreaction. It is the correct response to a parent's unease.

7. Why Direct Asking Matters so Much Here
Suicidal desire, in Thomas Joiner's framework, grows from two specific experiences: feeling that you do not belong anywhere, and feeling that you are a burden to the people who love you [1]. A child who has been bullied often has both, and often quietly. Direct questions are how those two things become visible in time to act [2].

8. Next Steps
- Ask directly. Today. In plain words.
- Do not leave them alone afterward.
- Remove access to anything they could use to hurt themselves, without discussing the specifics with them [13].
- Call a crisis line or your child's physician the same day, and follow their guidance.
- If any self-harm or suicide attempt has already happened, get medical evaluation immediately. Call 911 or go to the nearest emergency room. Physical injuries — including ones that look minor, and including any overdose — need a medical assessment, because some risks are not visible and some appear hours later. Do not wait to see how they seem.
- Tell one adult who needs to know — your child's other parent, or the person who shares care of them.

9. 🌟 Love Light Moment
A child who says they don't want to be here is not telling you they've decided. They are telling you the pain has outgrown the words they have for it.
Most young people who die by suicide show warning signs or changes in behavior, though not all do. Often the sign is quiet — a child telling a parent, at night, in a voice that sounds tired rather than dramatic.
That is not the end of something. It is the beginning of the part where someone finally knows.
Nothing has been "fixed."
But your child is not alone in it anymore, and that changes what happens next.

1. The Situation — Longer
Your child has said something that opened a door you weren't prepared to walk through. There are many forms this takes, and parents usually under-recognize all of them. Sometimes it's explicit — your child says they don't want to be here anymore, or that everyone would be better off without them. Sometimes it's indirect: a remark about not being around for a milestone, an unusual calm after a long stretch of despair, giving away possessions, withdrawing from everything they used to love, a sudden interest in saying goodbye to people. Sometimes it arrives as writing, or a playlist, or a comment to a sibling. And something in you registers that this is different from the other hard moments in this arc. That recognition is worth trusting. Many parents who have lost a child describe, looking back, having had a moment when they already knew — and then talking themselves out of it. Do not talk yourself out of it.

2. Why This Needs a Different Response Than Everything Else in This Library
Every other guide in this library is about a child being hurt by someone else, and the work is teaching your child to stand in it. This one is different in kind. When suicidal thinking is present, the threat to your child is not coming from outside any more, and the parent's role changes from coach to safety anchor. That's why this guide is shorter on scripts and longer on sequence. The scripts matter less than doing the right things in the right order [2]. And the sequence is: ask, stay, get help today.

3. Understanding What's Actually Happening — The Two Ingredients
The most influential research framework for understanding suicidal thinking comes from Thomas Joiner, whose interpersonal theory identifies two elements that create the desire for suicide: thwarted belongingness — the sense of not being connected to or accepted by anyone — and perceived burdensomeness — the belief that one's existence is a burden on others, and that others would be better off without them [1]. Joiner's framework also distinguishes the desire to die from the capability to act on it, which is why those two ingredients can be present in a child who would never act — and why they must still be taken seriously [1].

Read that against everything else in this library and it becomes very hard to ignore. A child who has been bullied for months has often been given both ingredients. Exclusion builds thwarted belongingness directly [9][10]. Being told you're unwelcome, unlikable, or a problem builds perceived burdensomeness directly [10]. That is why bullying is not a separate topic from suicide risk in young people, and why a guide in this library has to exist in the first place [11].

David Jobes's collaborative approach adds something essential for parents: children and adolescents who are suicidal usually have their own reasons, their own story about what's driving it, and those reasons need to be heard rather than corrected [2]. Jobes's framework treats the act of understanding the person's own explanation as part of the treatment, not a preliminary to it [2]. For you, tonight, that means: when your child tells you why, do not argue with the reasons. Receive them. You are not doing therapy. You are keeping them alive until a professional can.

4. How to Ask — In Detail
There is an understandable fear that naming suicide introduces it. The evidence does not support that fear. A meta-analysis covering thirteen studies found that inquiring about suicidal risk did not increase suicidal behavior or psychological distress, and school-based screening research has found no evidence of harm from asking [4]. Asking is not dangerous. Asking is the tool.

Do it plainly and without drama. Sit down. Give yourself a moment to sound like yourself.

"I need to ask you something, and I'm not going to be upset with the answer. Are you thinking about ending your life?"

Then stop talking. The silence after the question is part of the question. A child may need thirty seconds to answer, and many will test whether you can tolerate the silence before they say anything true.

Some things that get in the way: softening the question into something vague like "you're not thinking about doing anything stupid, are you?" — which signals your discomfort and gives them an easy exit. Asking while distracted, or half-turned away. Asking and then filling the silence yourself. Asking in a tone that says please say no. They will hear that tone and say no.

If you can't manage it in the moment, that's not a failure of love. Call a crisis line and ask them to walk you through it. They do this constantly [5].

5. If They Say Yes
Your job in the next several hours is not to make them feel better. It is to keep them alive and get them to professional care. Say less. Say these four things and then stop:

  • "Thank you for telling me. I know that was hard."
  • "I'm not going anywhere."
  • "You are not in trouble, and you're not going to be alone in this."
  • "We're going to get help today. Not next week. Today."

Then stay with them. Do not leave them alone — not to make a phone call in the other room, not to sleep in your own bed tonight. If you must make a call, do it within their sight or earshot.

Do not promise secrecy. When they ask you to tell no one, the honest and safe answer is: "I can't keep this one to myself, because you're too important to me. But I'll tell you every single thing I do, before I do it." That preserves the trust without accepting a promise you must not keep.

Call for guidance. A crisis line will help you decide whether this can be handled at home with a same-day professional appointment, or whether you're looking at an emergency evaluation [5]. That is a clinical judgment, not a parent's judgment, and the person on the line is trained to make it with you.

If the answer is an emergency evaluation, that does not mean you did anything wrong. The phrase can land like a verdict on your parenting. It is not. It is a description of how much support is needed right now. The counselor on the line will tell you where to go and what to bring, so ask them plainly: what happens when we get there, and how long does it take. You do not have to work out the threshold yourself, and you do not have to decide whether this counts. That decision gets made with you, by someone trained to make it.

If any attempt or self-harm has already happened, this is not a judgment call — get medical evaluation now. Call 911 or go to the nearest emergency room. Injuries can look minor and still need assessment, and an overdose of any kind needs medical attention even if your child seems fine afterward, because some effects show up hours later. Do not wait to see how they seem.

What happens at a medical evaluation, so you are not walking in blind. Most parents and children fear this part simply because they do not know what it is. Broadly: your child is seen by a clinician, often in an emergency department or a crisis assessment service. They will ask about what your child has been thinking and feeling, and about what has been happening at home and at school. There may be a physical check, particularly if any self-harm has occurred. You will be asked what you have observed. It is a conversation and an assessment, not an interrogation, and you are allowed to ask questions at any point — including what are you looking for, what happens next, and how long will this take.

The outcome is usually one of three things: your child is safe to go home with a same-day or next-day plan and follow-up; or they are referred for urgent outpatient care with specific next steps; or they are admitted for observation and stabilization. Being admitted is not a punishment and not proof you failed. It means the level of support needed right now is more than can be provided at home. You are allowed to ask which outcome they are considering and why, and you are allowed to say you want to understand the plan before you leave. Bringing your child's medications, any written notes about what you have seen, and a list of people who can help can make the visit go more smoothly.

6. If They Say No
Children sometimes deny suicidal thinking because they're frightened of what your response will cost them — an emergency room, a locked unit, having to watch you fall apart. A "no" delivered quickly, in a flat voice, while avoiding your eyes, is not reassurance.

Seek help even after a denial if concerns remain. If your instinct says the answer was not honest, act on the instinct. Seek help even after a denial, if concerns remain. You do not need their admission to call a professional for guidance. Calling a crisis line to say "my child told me something and is now denying it — what should I do?" is exactly the right use of that resource. And it is not an overreaction. Nobody on that line will tell you that you worried too much [5].

7. Reducing Access — Said Carefully and Generally
One of the most consistent findings in suicide prevention is that putting time and distance between a person in crisis and the means to harm themselves saves lives [13]. This is stated generally here on purpose: this guide will not describe methods, and if you find yourself wanting specifics, that is a conversation to have with a professional today, not with an article. In practice, this means reducing access to anything in your home that could be used for self-harm, and doing it without a negotiation. You do not need your child's agreement. You need them to survive the next few days.

8. What Helps That You Might Not Expect
Sleep and time — but only when professional support is in place. Suicidal crisis is often acute and time-limited. If you can get your child safely through the next forty-eight hours with professional support already in place, the arithmetic changes [2]. Read that sentence carefully: it is a reason to get help today and then hold on, not a reason to wait and see. Distress can ease when a person stays alive, stays supervised, and stays connected to care — the relief comes from the support, not from the passing of time. Do not read this as let's see how they feel tomorrow. Get help today, and let time do its work alongside it.
Connection, specifically. Joiner's framework names belonging as one of the two ingredients, which means belonging is also part of the treatment [1]. Not a speech about how loved they are — a person. Someone who shows up. If there is an aunt, a cousin, a coach, a friend who your child genuinely likes, that person's presence matters [1].
Bringing them in rather than managing them. Jobes's collaborative approach works because the person in crisis is engaged as a participant in their own safety planning rather than being done to [2]. Ask your child what would help. Then listen, and act on what you can.
Writing it down. A safety plan on paper — people to call, places to go, things that helped before — is more useful than an agreement made verbally in a hard moment. A crisis counselor will help you build one [5].

9. What Does Not Help
Being told it will pass. True, usually, and it can land as your pain is an inconvenience.
Being reminded of what they have to live for. A person in this state has already measured that. Arguments about it read as not understanding.
Secrecy. If your child asks you to hold this, you cannot. Say so kindly, and tell them what you'll do instead.
Waiting for a better moment. There isn't one, and the window is often short.
Handling it alone. You are a parent, not a clinician, and no parent should carry this by themselves in a quiet house at midnight. Call [5].
Reading more articles instead of making the call. Including this one. Finish this paragraph, then pick up the phone.

10. The Longer Road
Getting through tonight is the first thing. What comes after tonight is also real, and it needs a professional [2]. Ask your child's doctor for a referral to a clinician who works with adolescents. Ask whether a safety plan exists and whether one should be built. Ask what a follow-up schedule looks like, and what warning signs would mean going back in sooner.

And then, when the crisis has passed, return to the rest of this library. A child who has been bullied for a long time needs what the earlier guides offer: a parent who listens, who asks before advising, who builds the conditions rather than reacting to the events. That work matters more than it sounds. Belonging is not a soft concept in this arena. Per Joiner's framework, it is one of the two things standing between a child and the deepest form of hopelessness [1], and it is built at dinner tables, in cars, and in the ordinary thousands of small moments a parent gets with a child who is still here.

Getting Help Now

If you believe your child is in immediate danger, call 911 or go to your nearest emergency room.

988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline — Call or text 988, any time, day or night. Free and confidential, across the United States and territories. Chat is also available online. Spanish speakers can text AYUDA to 988 [5].

Crisis Text Line — Text HOME to 741741 to reach a trained crisis counselor by text message [6].

The Trevor Project — Call 1-866-488-7386 or text START to 678678. Crisis support specifically for LGBTQ+ young people [7].

Childhelp National Child Abuse Hotline — Call or text 1-800-422-4453. For suspected abuse or neglect [8]. This is a support line, not a reporting line. Calling it does not file a report and does not notify authorities or 911.

Your child's pediatrician or family doctor — Same-day or next-day appointments can often be arranged if you explain that your child has expressed thoughts of self-harm.

You do not have to know what to say before you call. Saying "my child told me something tonight and I don't know what to do" is a complete sentence, and it is the sentence these services are built for.
